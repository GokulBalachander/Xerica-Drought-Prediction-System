About Us

Drought, seen a week ahead

Drought builds slowly, then hits farms, water supplies and ecosystems all at once. The U.S. Drought Monitor maps where drought is each week, but by the time a county turns red on that map, the damage has already started. Xerica forecasts next week's drought map, so water managers, farmers and communities get a head start instead of a late warning.

Methodology

Input · Drought history

U.S. Drought Monitor maps

Every week the Drought Monitor releases a national map that sorts land into five categories, D0 Abnormally Dry through D4 Exceptional. Xerica learns from the sequence of these maps over time: how far drought areas move, grow and recede from one week to the next.

Input · From orbit

Satellite and soil moisture data

Satellite imagery shows how green and healthy vegetation is, and soil moisture shows how much water is left in the ground. Browning plants and drying soil often show up before a drought is officially declared, which makes them early warning signs for the model.

Input · Weather and climate

Climate indicators

Rainfall, temperature and related climate indicators describe how much water is falling compared with how much heat is pulling it away. Weeks of below-normal rain combined with above-normal heat push a region toward drought, so these signals help the model judge which way conditions are heading.

Output · Forecast

Neural network forecast

A neural network built in Keras combines all three inputs and predicts next week's drought category for every pixel of the map. The forecast is snapped to the Drought Monitor's weekly release dates and exported as GeoJSON, so it opens directly in ArcGIS, QGIS and other mapping tools.
